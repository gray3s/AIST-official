SHORT ANSWERS
-------------
Question 1 -- Is the OP's response true?
   Mostly, but with one materially wrong sentence and one important omission.
   The memory arithmetic is exactly right. The claim that "beyond ~45 qubits no
   amount of classical hardware can fake it" is FALSE as stated -- classical
   simulability depends on the *circuit*, not the qubit count, and every single
   circuit the OP actually ran is classically easy at thousands of qubits. The
   IBM cloud-access claim is true with caveats.

Question 2 -- Can a 32-qubit online simulator mimic a real 2048-qubit system via
a FEM-like algorithm or any other algorithm?
   In general, no -- and the reason is not memory layout, it is entanglement
   entropy plus complexity theory. But the FEM instinct is a *good* one: the
   quantum analogue of FEM already exists (tensor networks / MPS-DMRG), it is
   the state of the art, and it does push far past 32 qubits -- for circuits
   whose "solution is smooth." For a 2048-qubit circuit doing something worth
   doing, no such method can work, and the proof is almost tautological: if it
   could, nobody would be spending billions on the hardware.

VERIFIED CORRECT
----------------
1. 69 GB for 32 qubits. 2^32 = 4,294,967,296 amplitudes. At complex128
   (16 bytes: two float64s) that is 68,719,476,736 bytes = 68.72 GB decimal,
   which is *exactly* 64 GiB. "69 GB" is right.

2. Doubling per qubit, and the specific ceilings:
      33 qubits -> 137.4 GB = exactly 128 GiB (i.e. the entire nominal RAM of a
                   "128 GB" MacBook, so it genuinely will not fit)
      40 qubits -> 17.59 TB   ("18 terabytes" -- correct)
      50 qubits -> 18.01 PB   ("more than most data centers" -- correct)
   The exponential-wall framing is legitimate and well stated.

3. "32 independent coins would agree once in about 2.1 billion flips."
   P(all same) = 2 x (1/2)^32 = 2^-31 = 1 in 2,147,483,648. Correct.

4. The 488 / 512 split. For a GHZ state, n=1000, p=0.5, the standard deviation
   is sqrt(250) = 15.8. A 488/512 split is 0.76 sigma from center -- exactly
   what an honest run looks like. (A suspiciously clean 500/500 would have been
   more of a red flag.)

5. Grover: 4 queries for N=32. floor((pi/4) x sqrt(32)) = floor(4.44) = 4.
   Correct. Classical expected queries = (32+1)/2 = 16.5, so "16" is fair.

6. 71 seconds is plausible, which is a point in the OP's favor for authenticity.
   A 32-qubit GHZ needs 1 Hadamard + 31 CNOTs; each two-qubit gate is a full
   streaming pass over the state (read + write ~137 GB). 32 gates x 137 GB
   = ~4.4 TB of memory traffic. An M3/M4 Max sustains roughly 400 GB/s, giving
   ~11 s of pure bandwidth, plus allocation, zeroing, and 1000 samplings. 71 s
   is the right order of magnitude. The numbers hang together.

7. NIST has standardized post-quantum replacements. True: FIPS 203 (ML-KEM,
   from CRYSTALS-Kyber), FIPS 204 (ML-DSA, from CRYSTALS-Dilithium), FIPS 205
   (SLH-DSA, from SPHINCS+), finalized August 2024, with HQC later selected as
   a backup KEM. The OP's practical advice -- start PQC migration planning now,
   independent of the hardware timeline -- is the soundest claim in the post.

8. Shor factoring 15 into 3 x 5. Genuinely doable in a full simulator.

THE CENTRAL PROBLEM WITH THE DEMONSTRATION
------------------------------------------
The 32-coin experiment, exactly as described, demonstrates nothing quantum.

The reported data is: 1000 measurements, all 32 bits agreeing every time,
~50/50 between all-zeros and all-ones. That distribution is reproduced perfectly
by the most boring classical program imaginable:

      flip one fair coin, then copy the result into 32 registers.

That is a local hidden-variable model. It has no entanglement, needs no
superposition, and fits in 32 bits of RAM instead of 69 GB. The "1 in 2.1
billion" astonishment is doing rhetorical work it hasn't earned -- the coins are
not independent in the classical model either, because they were copied from one
source.

What separates a GHZ state from a broadcast classical bit is *measuring in more
than one basis*. Correlations in Z alone are classically trivial. The quantum
signature only appears when you measure in X/Y bases as well and find
correlations that no shared-randomness model can reproduce -- the Mermin/GHZ
paradox (a deterministic, all-or-nothing contradiction with local realism) or a
CHSH/Bell inequality violation. The OP measured one basis, 1000 times.

This also means the appeal to Einstein and "spooky action at a distance" is
misplaced twice over:
   (a) The data shown is exactly what a *non-spooky* local model predicts.
   (b) A simulation cannot test nonlocality at all. Nonlocality is a statement
       about spacelike-separated measurements on physical systems. A single
       laptop computing a state vector has no separation to violate. You can
       simulate the *formalism* of entanglement; you cannot demonstrate the
       *phenomenon*. That takes photons and distance.

Similarly, "no qubit had a value until the moment of measurement" is an
interpretive gloss, not an experimental finding. Inside the program, every
amplitude had a fully determined value at every instant, and the measurement
outcome came from a classical pseudorandom number generator with a seed. What
was demonstrated is that the Born rule was implemented correctly.

None of this makes the exercise worthless -- taking the linear algebra apart by
hand is a genuinely good way to learn the subject, and the OP's cost/effort
claims are honest. But the framing oversells what the run establishes.

TWO SMALLER OVERSTATEMENTS
--------------------------
* Grover "found 1 marked item out of 32." You had to write the oracle, which
  means you encoded the answer before searching for it. This is the standard
  caveat on every Grover demo; the speedup is real in the query model but no
  information was actually discovered. (Also worth noting: 32 items = 5 qubits,
  not 32 -- easy to conflate with the other experiment.)

* Shor on 15. In simulation this can be done properly. On real hardware, nearly
  every historical "we factored N" result used a circuit compiled with knowledge
  of the answer, a critique made forcefully by Smolin, Smith and Vargo
  ("Oversimplifying quantum factoring"). Worth keeping straight when comparing
  simulator results to hardware press releases.

================================================================================
PART 2 -- QUESTION 1: IS THE OP'S REPLY TRUE?
================================================================================

The OP made four claims. Scored individually:

CLAIM A: "The simulation uses exact physics."
   MISLEADING. It uses exact *linear algebra* on an idealized noiseless model.
   The simulation is cleaner than any real device -- no decoherence, no gate
   infidelity, no crosstalk, no leakage, no readout error. It is exact with
   respect to the textbook, not with respect to nature. A real 32-qubit machine
   would produce visibly worse GHZ data than the laptop did.

CLAIM B: "My Mac tops out at 32 qubits."
   TRUE ONLY FOR DENSE STATE-VECTOR SIMULATION. This is the crux, and it is
   where the OP's argument quietly assumes its conclusion. The 69 GB is an
   artifact of one method -- storing all 2^n amplitudes explicitly.

   The GHZ circuit the OP ran is a *Clifford* circuit (Hadamard + CNOTs). By the
   Gottesman-Knill theorem, Clifford circuits are simulable exactly in O(n^2)
   time and space using a stabilizer tableau. Aaronson and Gottesman's CHP, or
   Stim today, will produce that identical 488/512 result for 32 qubits in
   microseconds and in a few kilobytes -- and will do 100,000 qubits on the same
   laptop before lunch.

   As a tensor network, a GHZ state has bond dimension 2. A 32-qubit GHZ as an
   MPS is a few hundred bytes.

   Grover with a single marked item is also cheap: every intermediate state is a
   rank-2 combination of the marked basis state and the uniform superposition,
   so the bond dimension stays tiny. MPS handles it at hundreds of qubits.

   So the 69 GB was not a law of nature -- it was a tool choice. The one circuit
   in the set that genuinely resists compression is Shor's (the modular
   exponentiation and QFT generate real entanglement and a large non-Clifford
   gate count), and even there MPS methods have been pushed well past
   state-vector limits.

CLAIM C: "Beyond ~45 qubits, no amount of classical hardware can fake it."
   FALSE AS STATED. This is the one sentence that should be retracted.

   The ~45-qubit figure is right for exact dense state-vector simulation
   (Haener & Steiger reached 45 qubits in 2017 on Cori II using roughly half a
   petabyte across 8192 nodes; compression tricks have nudged this to the high
   40s / low 60s at reduced fidelity). But classical simulability is not a
   function of qubit count. It is a function of the circuit's entanglement
   structure, its non-Clifford ("magic") content, its depth, and its noise
   level. Counterexamples, all real:

     - Clifford circuits: thousands of qubits, exact, poly cost.
     - Clifford + few T gates: cost scales with T-count, not qubit count.
     - Low-entanglement (area-law) circuits: MPS/PEPS handle hundreds to
       thousands of qubits.
     - Shallow / structured circuits: tensor-network contraction has been used
       at 60-120+ qubits.
     - Noisy circuits: this is the most damaging counterexample to the OP's
       framing. IBM's much-publicized 127-qubit "quantum utility" experiment
       (Kim et al., Nature 2023) was matched or beaten within months by purely
       classical methods -- tensor networks (Tindall et al.) and sparse Pauli
       dynamics / Pauli-path propagation (Begusic & Chan). Subsequent work has
       shown that noise itself makes broad classes of circuits classically
       simulable, because noise damps the long Pauli paths that carry the hard
       part of the computation.

   The defensible version of Claim C is: "There *exist* circuits on 50-100
   qubits that no classical hardware can simulate, and we do not know how to
   tell in advance which ones." That is still a strong claim and still justifies
   building the hardware. But "beyond 45, classical is dead" is not right, and
   the noisy-circuit results cut against hardware-advantage claims specifically.

CLAIM D: "IBM gives free cloud access to actual 100+ qubit processors; the
front-end is the same 20 lines of Python."
   TRUE, WITH CAVEATS. IBM's open plan does provide free access to 100+ qubit
   devices (127-qubit Eagle, and the 133/156-qubit Heron generation). The Qiskit
   front-end really is nearly identical -- swap the Aer simulator backend for a
   hardware backend. Caveats worth stating:
     - Free-tier time is capped (on the order of ~10 minutes of QPU time per
       month) and queues can be long.
     - Results are *noisy*. On a real 127-qubit device, a naive 32-qubit GHZ will
       not give 1000/1000 agreement; expect meaningful mixed-outcome counts from
       decoherence and readout error. Getting clean results requires error
       mitigation, transpilation tuning, and connectivity-aware layout.
     - The OP's own rhetorical setup gets inverted here: the simulator is the
       thing that produced the perfect textbook data. The real machine is the
       messy one.

NET VERDICT ON THE OP'S REPLY
   The conclusion -- "you still need to build real quantum computers" -- is
   correct. The argument offered for it is the weakest available one, because it
   rests on a memory-footprint accident of a single simulation technique rather
   than on the actual barrier, which is computational complexity. And it is
   stated with a false universal ("no amount of classical hardware").

PART 3 -- QUESTION 2: CAN A FEM-LIKE ALGORITHM LET 32 QUBITS MIMIC 2048?
================================================================================

3.1 FIRST, A TERMINOLOGY DISAMBIGUATION
---------------------------------------
"2048 qubits" and "RSA-2048" are different things and it is worth separating
them, since the OP's post invoked RSA:

  * 2048 *physical* qubits already exist and are not the frontier. D-Wave's
    annealers passed 2000 qubits in 2017 and are now in the 5000+ range (though
    annealers are not universal gate-model machines and are far more
    classically tractable). Neutral-atom platforms have gone from ~1180 atoms
    (Atom Computing, 2023) to multi-thousand-atom arrays. Raw qubit count is
    the easy axis.

  * 2048 *logical*, error-corrected qubits do not exist. Current state of the
    art is single-digit to low-double-digit logical qubits (Google's Willow
    below-threshold surface-code result in 2024; Quantinuum/Microsoft's ~12
    logical qubits; QuEra's 48 logical qubits on neutral atoms). Each logical
    qubit costs roughly 10^2-10^3 physical qubits.

  * Breaking RSA-2048 is a *different* number again. Gidney & Ekera's 2021
    estimate was ~20 million noisy physical qubits for 8 hours; Gidney's 2025
    revision brought that under 1 million physical qubits for under a week.
    Enormous progress on the estimate -- and still two to three orders of
    magnitude beyond any existing device.

So "mimic a real 2048-qubit system" is asking about a machine that has not been
built. The interesting version of the question is the one asked: is the
exponential wall a *representation* problem that a smarter discretization could
dissolve?

3.2 WHY THE FEM ANALOGY IS THE RIGHT INSTINCT -- AND EXACTLY WHERE IT BREAKS
----------------------------------------------------------------------------
FEM is spectacularly effective because of a specific structural gift:

  The domain has FIXED, LOW dimension (d = 1, 2, or 3), and the solution is
  SMOOTH (elliptic regularity), so a small number of local basis functions
  captures it. Degrees of freedom scale as O(h^-d) -- polynomial in refinement,
  with d held constant.

Quantum state space does not have that gift:

  The Hilbert space dimension is 2^n. There is no fixed low-dimensional domain
  to mesh. The effective dimensionality *grows with the number of qubits*.

This is the classic curse of dimensionality, and it is worth being blunt about
the scale: FEM and its relatives already become impractical for PDEs at d = 10,
which is why high-dimensional problems are handed to Monte Carlo instead. The
question is asking about d = 2048. The state vector alone would require ~10^618
bytes. There are roughly 10^80 atoms in the observable universe. This is not a
hardware gap, an engineering gap, or a Moore's-law gap. It is a different
category of impossibility.

Mapping the analogy term by term:

    FINITE ELEMENT METHOD              QUANTUM CIRCUIT SIMULATION
    ---------------------------------  ------------------------------------
    Mesh elements / basis functions    Tensor-network bond dimension chi
    Mesh refinement h -> 0             chi -> 2^(n/2)  (exact limit)
    Elliptic regularity / smoothness   Area-law entanglement
    Galerkin projection error          Truncation of Schmidt coefficients
    Adaptive refinement at a shock     chi blowup as entanglement grows
    Turbulence: DNS infeasible         Volume-law entanglement: exact
                                       simulation infeasible
    Fixed domain dimension d = 3       Effective dimension = n (grows!)

The last row is the whole answer. Every other row shows the analogy working.
That one row shows why it cannot be pushed to 2048.

3.3 THE QUANTUM FEM ALREADY EXISTS: TENSOR NETWORKS
---------------------------------------------------
The single most useful thing to know here is that the FEM-like approach is not
hypothetical -- it is the dominant tool in the field, and it is structurally the
same idea: replace the full space with a variational manifold parameterized by a
refinement knob.

  * Matrix Product States (MPS) / DMRG -- the 1D workhorse. Bond dimension chi
    is the refinement parameter. Cost is O(n x chi^3) instead of O(2^n). For
    area-law states, chi stays bounded and you simulate *thousands* of qubits
    on a laptop. This is precisely "FEM for quantum."
  * PEPS / 2D tensor networks -- for 2D lattices; contraction is harder
    (formally #P-hard in general) but approximable.
  * Tree tensor networks, MERA -- other topologies for other entanglement
    structures.
  * Contraction-order optimization (e.g. cotengra) -- for shallow circuits,
    treat the circuit as a tensor network and find a good contraction path.
    This is what let classical machines catch up to Google's 53-qubit Sycamore
    supremacy claim.

And the complementary family, which trades a different resource:

  * Stabilizer / Clifford tableau (Gottesman-Knill) -- exact, poly, thousands
    of qubits. Not universal.
  * Stabilizer rank / Clifford+T decomposition -- cost roughly 2^(0.5 t) in the
    T-gate count t. Universal, and the exponential moves from n to t.
  * Schrodinger-Feynman hybrid / circuit cutting -- slice the circuit, trade
    memory for time exponential in the number of cuts.
  * Feynman path summation -- O(1) memory, exponential time in gate count.
  * Sparse Pauli dynamics / Pauli-path propagation (Heisenberg picture) --
    propagate observables instead of states, truncate small Pauli terms. This is
    the method that matched IBM's 127-qubit experiment.
  * Neural-network quantum states -- RBM / transformer ansatze as a learned
    compression of the wavefunction. Directly analogous to a data-driven
    reduced-order model.
  * "Dequantization" (Ewin Tang and successors) -- for quantum machine-learning
    claims resting on low-rank linear algebra, classical sampling-based
    algorithms achieve comparable scaling, removing the claimed speedup
    entirely.

Every one of these can, on the right problem, blow past 32 qubits by orders of
magnitude. So the answer to "is there any algorithm that does better than dense
state vector?" is emphatically yes, and the OP should know about them.

3.4 WHY NONE OF THEM SOLVES THE STATED PROBLEM
----------------------------------------------
Each method above buys its efficiency by assuming structure. State the
assumption and the failure mode becomes obvious:

    METHOD                  ASSUMES                  FAILS WHEN
    ----------------------  -----------------------  -------------------------
    Stabilizer tableau      Clifford-only gates      Any T / Toffoli / QFT
    Stabilizer rank         Few non-Clifford gates   Shor, Grover (huge T count)
    MPS / DMRG              Area-law entanglement    Volume-law entanglement
    PEPS                    2D locality              Long-range / all-to-all
    Shallow contraction     Low depth                Deep circuits
    Circuit cutting         Few cuts suffice         Highly connected circuits
    Pauli-path truncation   Noise or shallow depth   Coherent deep circuits
    Dequantization          Low-rank input data      General unitary dynamics

A 2048-qubit computation worth running is, by definition, one that is *not*
efficiently classically simulable -- otherwise you would just run the classical
method and skip the refrigerator. Shor's algorithm at cryptographic scale is
deliberately maximal on every axis in the right-hand column: deep, globally
entangling via the QFT, enormous non-Clifford count, and volume-law entanglement
through modular exponentiation. It sits precisely in the gap that every
FEM-like ansatz leaves open.

3.5 THE ARGUMENT THAT SETTLES IT
--------------------------------
There is a cleaner way to see this than enumerating methods, and it does not
depend on any of them:

  Suppose a FEM-like algorithm existed that let a modest classical machine
  faithfully reproduce the output distribution of an arbitrary 2048-qubit
  quantum circuit, with polynomial resources.

  Then BQP would be contained in (roughly) P or BPP. Shor's algorithm would run
  classically, meaning RSA and ECC would already be broken by that algorithm
  alone -- no quantum hardware required, and no post-quantum migration would
  help, since the break would be classical. Simultaneously, every quantum
  advantage would evaporate and the entire multi-billion-dollar hardware effort
  would be pointless.

  Exact simulation is stronger still: computing output amplitudes of general
  quantum circuits is #P-hard, and additive-error sampling from them collapses
  the polynomial hierarchy under standard assumptions.

The existence of an efficient classical mimic and the usefulness of quantum
hardware are the *same question* with opposite signs. You cannot have both. So
"just use a simulator instead of building the machines" is not merely
impractical -- it is self-refuting. If the shortcut worked, there would be
nothing on the other side of it to reach.

3.6 WHAT SIMULATORS ARE ACTUALLY FOR
------------------------------------
This is where the original intuition ("maybe we don't need to build them") lands
on something true and useful, just not the thing it aimed at. Simulators are
indispensable -- every hardware group runs them constantly -- as:

  * algorithm development and debugging, with full state visibility that no
    real device can offer
  * verification: a classically-checkable ground truth to validate hardware
    output on small instances
  * noise modeling and error-correction design (Stim exists precisely for
    simulating enormous Clifford-heavy QEC circuits)
  * establishing the classical baseline -- the moving line that any quantum
    advantage claim must clear, which is why the tensor-network and Pauli-path
    results above matter so much

They are a development and verification tool. They are not a substitute, and the
sharper point is that they *define* what a substitute would have to beat.

================================================================================
PART 4 -- BOTTOM LINE
================================================================================

1. The OP's arithmetic is correct throughout: 69 GB at 32 qubits, doubling per
   qubit, 18 TB at 40, 18 PB at 50, 1-in-2.1-billion, 4 Grover queries. The
   71-second runtime is consistent with memory-bandwidth limits, which supports
   the claim that the run was real.

2. The headline demonstration does not show what it claims. Z-basis-only
   all-agree data on a GHZ state is reproduced exactly by copying one classical
   coin flip into 32 registers. Showing entanglement requires multiple
   measurement bases (Mermin/GHZ or CHSH); showing nonlocality requires
   spacelike-separated physical measurements, which no simulation can do.

3. The 69 GB was a tool choice, not a wall. GHZ and single-target Grover are
   both classically trivial -- Clifford tableau or bond-dimension-2 MPS -- at
   thousands of qubits on the same laptop. Only Shor's circuit in that set
   genuinely resists compression.

4. "Beyond ~45 qubits no classical hardware can fake it" is false. Simulability
   tracks entanglement, magic, depth, and noise -- not qubit count. Noisy
   circuits in particular have proven far more classically tractable than
   expected, as the IBM 127-qubit utility episode showed.

5. A 32-qubit simulator cannot mimic a general 2048-qubit machine by any
   algorithm. FEM works because the domain dimension is fixed at 3 and the
   solution is smooth; here the effective dimension grows with qubit count, and
   at n = 2048 the state vector would need ~10^618 bytes against ~10^80 atoms in
   the universe.

6. The FEM instinct is nonetheless correct in form. Tensor networks are exactly
   that idea -- bond dimension as mesh refinement, area law as smoothness -- and
   they are the state of the art, reaching thousands of qubits when the
   entanglement cooperates. They fail on precisely the circuits that make
   quantum hardware worth building.

7. The decisive argument is a dilemma, not a resource count: an efficient
   classical mimic of arbitrary quantum circuits would break RSA classically and
   render all quantum hardware pointless in one stroke. The shortcut cannot
   exist without destroying the destination.

8. The OP's practical recommendation stands and is the most actionable item in
   the whole exchange: begin post-quantum cryptography migration now. The
   standards are final (FIPS 203/204/205), the driver is harvest-now-decrypt-
   later against long-lived secrets, and migration timelines for large
   organizations run to years -- so the decision does not depend on resolving
   the hardware-timeline disagreement at all.
